## 1. The Science — ENSO mechanics, what makes "super," and what 2026 actually looks like

### 1.1 What ENSO is (in 200 words)

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation is a coupled ocean-atmosphere oscillation in the tropical Pacific. In the *neutral* state, easterly trade winds blow surface water from South America toward Indonesia, piling warm water in the western Pacific (the "Warm Pool," 29-30°C) and allowing cold deep water to upwell off Peru (the "cold tongue," ~22-24°C). The atmospheric expression of this — rising air over the warm west, sinking air over the cool east — is called the **Walker Circulation**.

In **El Niño**, the trade winds slacken. The piled-up warm water sloshes east. Sea surface temperatures (SSTs) in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific rise. The Walker cell weakens or reverses. Deep convection (thunderstorm activity) shifts from Indonesia eastward toward the dateline. Because the tropical Pacific is the planet's largest single heat reservoir, that displacement reorganizes the jet streams and ripples globally — drought in Indonesia/Australia/Northeast Brazil/Southeast Africa, floods in Peru/Ecuador, weakened Indian monsoon, suppressed Atlantic hurricanes, stronger Pacific typhoons, warm winters in North America.

**La Niña** is the mirror — strengthened trades, intensified cold tongue, drought in southern US, wet in Australia/Indonesia.

### 1.2 The thresholds (this matters for the "super" claim)

The standard measure is the **Oceanic Niño Index (ONI)**: a 3-month running mean of SST anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region (5°N-5°S, 170°W-120°W) relative to a rolling 30-year baseline.

| Classification | ONI threshold | Frequency since 1950 |
|---|---|---|
| Weak El Niño | +0.5 to +0.9°C | ~6 events |
| Moderate | +1.0 to +1.4°C | ~5 events |
| Strong | +1.5 to +1.9°C | 4 events (1972-73, 1987-88, 1991-92, 2002-03) |
| Very Strong / "Super" | ≥ +2.0°C | **3 events: 1982-83, 1997-98, 2015-16** |

The 2023-24 event peaked around +2.0°C (NOAA classified it Very Strong; some agencies were a hair below). So the "Super" club has 3-4 members in the satellite era.

**The tweet's "scale to today" framing of 1877 implicitly compares to a once-in-a-century-or-more event. The 2-in-3 NOAA probability for "Super" by fall is the right floor of plausibility, but a strong-but-not-super event (the modal outcome) won't produce 2024-cocoa-style moves across the board.**

### 1.3 What is actually happening in May 2026

Without live data access I won't fabricate ONI readings, but the structural setup is reasonable to reason about:

- **2023-24** was a Very Strong El Niño that peaked in Dec 2023.
- **2024-25** transitioned to a weak-to-moderate La Niña that persisted into 2025.
- **2025-26** should be a transition phase. ENSO oscillations have a 2-7 year periodicity; back-to-back Super El Niños are rare but not unprecedented (1982 and 1986-87 sit close together; 1997 to 2002 were dramatic).

For NOAA's CPC to be calling 2-in-3 odds of Super by fall, the *predictors* you'd want to see lit up are:
- **Warm Water Volume** above the climatological mean (this is the subsurface heat reservoir between 5°N-5°S and 120°E-80°W down to the 20°C isotherm — when it's anomalously warm, it's "loading the gun")
- **Downwelling equatorial Kelvin waves** propagating east (these are oceanic disturbances that deepen the thermocline as they cross the Pacific, surfacing warm water in the east)
- **Westerly Wind Bursts** in the western Pacific (the atmospheric trigger that initiates Kelvin waves)
- **Suppressed easterly trades** in the central Pacific (Niño 4 region)

When all four light up in MAM (March-April-May), the predictive skill for the following DJF (December-January-February) peak is genuinely high — into the 70-80% bucket for "El Niño develops" though "Super" is a much higher bar. The April-May months are also when ENSO models have historically been worst (the "spring predictability barrier"), so even a confident NOAA call carries a wide error band.

**Skepticism flag**: The claim "European models are even more aggressive" is true *as a pattern* — ECMWF and UK Met Office often run warmer than NOAA's NMME ensemble. But it's not unfalsifiable; the actual May 2026 forecast plumes have specific numbers. Anyone trading on this should read the latest **NOAA CPC ENSO Diagnostic Discussion** and the **IRI/CPC ENSO Forecast** directly, not the tweet.

### 1.4 Why "Super" matters more than "Strong"

The teleconnection magnitudes are not linear in ONI. There's a threshold-like response in several key channels:

- **Indian Summer Monsoon**: All-India rainfall correlates negatively with ENSO at about -0.5 in the Strong+ regime, but the *probability* of a "deficient" monsoon (<90% of long-term mean) jumps from ~30% in neutral years to ~50% in Strong and ~70% in Super.
- **Northeast Brazilian drought**: Reliably triggered by Strong+ El Niño; the agricultural cone (Mato Grosso/Goiás) is much less affected — actually often gets *more* rain.
- **Indonesian/Malaysian palm oil**: Rainfall deficits below 100mm/month for 3+ months cause oil palm flower abortion. The 18-24 month lag is because palm fruit takes that long to develop. So a 2026 super event hits 2027-2028 production.
- **Australian wheat**: Eastern Australia goes dry. WA wheat (90% of exports) is less correlated.
- **West African cocoa**: Subtle — the dominant mechanism is the harmattan winds and the Atlantic SST tripole, but Super El Niño correlates with dry Decembers in Ghana/Côte d'Ivoire.

A "Strong but not Super" 2026 El Niño would partially trigger these. A genuine Super event would trigger most simultaneously.

## 2. The 1877 analogy — what's right, what's misleading, what the body count actually proxied

### 2.1 The historical record

The 1876-1878 Great Famines (sometimes "Madras Famine" or "Great Drought of 1876-78") are real and devastating. Best academic estimates from Mike Davis (*Late Victorian Holocausts*, 2001), Cormac Ó Gráda (*Famine: A Short History*, 2009), and more recent quantitative work:

- **India**: 5.5-12 million deaths (Davis settled on ~10M; some recent re-estimates lower this to 7-8M)
- **China (Northern)**: 9-13 million deaths in the "Dingwu Famine" / "Incredible Famine" — Shanxi, Henan, Shaanxi, Shandong, Zhili
- **Brazil (Northeast)**: 500,000 - 2 million (the *Grande Seca*)
- **Other**: North Africa, Java, Pacific islands — hundreds of thousands

Honest range: **30-50 million** is defensible. The 50M number in the tweet is the upper end of credible estimates.

### 2.2 Why so many people died — and why "scale to today" is misleading

Davis's central argument in *Late Victorian Holocausts* is that **these were not pure climate disasters; they were political-economic catastrophes triggered by climate**. Key features:

- **Colonial extraction continued during famine.** India exported a *record* 6.4 million cwt of wheat to Britain during 1877-78. Lord Lytton (Viceroy) believed relief was "injurious" and explicitly prevented food redistribution.
- **No global emergency grain market.** Shipping was 30-60 day passages, no refrigeration, no futures markets to signal shortages.
- **Internal logistics were primitive.** India's rail network was 5,000 miles in 1877 vs ~75,000 today; China's was negligible. Grain couldn't move from surplus to deficit regions.
- **No surveillance, no satellite weather, no famine-early-warning system.** The Madras Presidency famine commission was set up *after* deaths.
- **Pre-Green Revolution yields.** Wheat in India was ~700 kg/ha in 1877 vs ~3,500 kg/ha today.
- **No irrigation infrastructure.** ~2% of cropland irrigated in 1877 vs ~50% in India and 22% globally today.
- **The Qing state was bankrupt** from the Taiping Rebellion (20-30M dead, 1850-64) and Second Opium War.

The 50M dead in 1877 weren't killed by a sea-surface-temperature anomaly. They were killed by a tropical weather pattern intersecting **colonialism, primitive logistics, primitive agronomy, and broken states**.

### 2.3 The "scale to today" inversion

"250 million equivalent" is wrong in the direction the tweet implies — modern systems should produce vastly fewer deaths, not vastly more. The right calibration:

| Buffer | 1877 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Global grain stocks (% of annual use) | ~5%? unmeasured | ~25-30% (wheat, rice) per FAO |
| Irrigated cropland | ~2% | ~22% |
| Rail/road network | ~5,000 mi (India) | ~75,000 mi (India); global container logistics |
| Wheat yield (India) | ~700 kg/ha | ~3,500 kg/ha |
| Emergency response | None | WFP, FAO, USAID, national reserves |
| Forecasting horizon | 0 | 6+ months on ENSO |

Realistic 2026 death tolls in a Super El Niño plus fertilizer shortage scenario, even with significant pessimism: **hundreds of thousands to low millions**, concentrated in fragile states (Sudan, Yemen, Somalia, Haiti, parts of the Sahel) where food import dependence + currency weakness + active conflict already produce famine conditions. Tragic, real, geopolitical — but a different order of magnitude than 50M.

**What this means for the trade**: the modal "El Niño + fertilizer crunch" scenario is not a Malthusian catastrophe. It's a **price spike + EM stress + corporate margin compression** — which is much more actionable as a trading thesis than the doom framing suggests.

## 3. The 1997-98 and 2015-16 base rates — the only modern comparables

### 3.1 1997-98 Super El Niño

ONI peaked +2.4°C in Nov 1997. Global impacts:

- **Indonesian fires** (driven by drought + slash-and-burn): displaced 20M people in haze; rough estimates $4-8B in damages.
- **Andean floods**: massive Peru/Ecuador damage.
- **Australian drought**: Eastern Australia wheat production fell ~25%.
- **Crop prices**: Wheat *fell* during the event (high global stocks). Coffee *rose* on Brazilian frost concerns (separate from ENSO). Cocoa was flat. Sugar rose ~30% then gave it back.

The 1997-98 lesson: even a textbook Super El Niño doesn't reliably move broad ag commodity prices in one direction. The signal was concentrated in regional, specific commodities (Indonesian palm, Australian wheat, Peruvian fish).

### 3.2 2015-16 Super El Niño

ONI peaked +2.6°C (the strongest on record by some metrics) in Nov 2015-Jan 2016.

| Commodity | Move (mid-2015 to late 2016) |
|---|---|
| Sugar (#11) | +90% (then gave back 30%) |
| Palm oil (CPO) | +50% |
| Cocoa | Flat to -10% |
| Wheat | -15% |
| Corn | -10% |
| Soybeans | +5% |
| Coffee (Arabica) | +25% |
| Rice | +5% |

Notice the pattern: **grains barely moved or fell** (global stocks were high). **Sugar, palm, and coffee — the soft commodities with concentrated supply — moved.** Cocoa was disappointing.

The 2015-16 lesson for trade construction:
- A broad ag basket (DBA) caught maybe +10-15% during the event.
- Single-commodity ETFs (SGG sugar, BAL cotton, JJG grains) had wildly different paths.
- The "tail" — the part where Ackman would have made his 100x — was sugar puts on cane mills, palm oil upside, or specific equity longs/shorts.

### 3.3 2023-24 Very Strong El Niño

The most recent event peaked Dec 2023 at +2.0°C. The crop-price legacy:

- **Cocoa**: 2,500 → 11,000+ $/MT (4×). Driven by Ghana/Côte d'Ivoire production collapse + cocoa swollen shoot virus + aging trees + dry harmattan. El Niño was one of several factors but the dominant one.
- **Coffee (Robusta)**: hit record highs in 2024 on Vietnamese drought. Arabica followed.
- **Sugar**: Spiked 40% then reversed.
- **Palm oil**: Range-bound — surprising given expectations.
- **Wheat/Corn/Soy**: Lower over 2024.
- **DBA**: Up ~10% over 2024.

The 2023-24 lesson: **cocoa was the lottery ticket, and it paid 4x in 9 months**. Sugar and coffee delivered modest convex returns. The grain complex was a trap.

If you're seeking the convex 2026-27 trade, **look at cocoa and coffee first, not corn and soy**.

## 4. The fertilizer / Hormuz claim — what's right, what's exaggerated

### 4.1 The seaborne fertilizer market

Global fertilizer is three nutrients:

**Nitrogen (N)** — largest by volume (~115Mt of urea-equivalent traded). Made from natural gas via the Haber-Bosch process. Major exporters: Russia, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Iran, Trinidad, Algeria. The Hormuz cluster (Saudi, Qatar, Iran, UAE) handles **~25-30% of seaborne urea**, not "a third of all fertilizer trade."

**Phosphate (P)** — ~30Mt of DAP/MAP traded. Major exporters: Morocco (40% of global reserves), Russia, China, Saudi Arabia, US. Hormuz is *not* a critical chokepoint for phosphate (Morocco ships from Casablanca/Jorf Lasfar).

**Potash (K)** — ~50Mt traded. Major exporters: Canada (Saskatchewan), Russia, Belarus, China. Hormuz is **irrelevant** to potash trade.

**The "1/3 of seaborne fertilizer trade" through Hormuz claim is overstated by roughly 2-3×.** The actual exposure is closer to 10-12% of all-nutrient seaborne fertilizer, concentrated in urea, with substitution possible (US, China, and Trinidad urea can step up; Russian urea routes via Baltic Sea or Black Sea).

### 4.2 The "US fertilizer at 75% of normal in mid-March" claim

This claim is suspicious. The USDA doesn't publish a "% of normal" fertilizer supply metric in real time. What USDA *does* track:

- **Domestic urea production**: at CF Industries, Nutrien, OCI plants — production was running normally as of last reads I have visibility into.
- **Imports**: yes, urea imports from Russia and Trinidad were disrupted by sanctions; CIS routes were reorganized.
- **Application timing**: spring 2025 (and presumably 2026) had weather-related logistical issues in some corn-belt states.
- **Prices**: NOLA urea has been elevated since 2022 but well below 2022 peaks.

A more defensible version of the claim: **US nitrogen fertilizer prices in spring 2026 are elevated relative to long-term mean, with import disruption from the Middle East adding 10-20% premium to spot prices, which affects 2026 application decisions and therefore 2026 yields.** That's actionable. "75% of normal" is not falsifiable enough to be useful.

### 4.3 The actual fertilizer-El Niño compounding

The tweet's strongest argument: **fertilizer inputs are decided in spring (T), but their yield effect is realized at harvest (T+5 months); El Niño weather impacts crops in T+6 to T+12 months; both compound on the same 2026 harvest and especially the 2027 harvest.**

This is correct in structure. The numerical magnitude:

- A 15-20% under-application of nitrogen in US corn typically reduces yields by 5-10% (Liebig's law of the minimum — diminishing marginal returns).
- A 10% US corn yield reduction = ~1.5 billion bushels = ~5% of global corn supply.
- Add a Brazilian Safrinha drought (El Niño teleconnection) of similar magnitude = another 2-3% global hit.
- Aggregate corn deficit of 7-10% is enough to move CBOT corn futures 30-50% in a single year (corn has the steepest demand inelasticity of major grains).

But here's the wrinkle: **El Niño typically helps Brazilian Safrinha (second-crop) corn**. It's *La Niña* that hurts Brazil. El Niño tends to give the US corn belt warmer winters and earlier springs, which can help yields. The pure-El-Niño thesis for corn is **weaker than for tropical-supplied softs**.

**For corn specifically, you want to be long fertilizer disruption, not long El Niño.**

For palm oil, cocoa, coffee, sugar — you want to be long El Niño regardless of fertilizer (these are tropical perennial crops that don't depend on US fertilizer markets).

## 5. The geopolitical landscape — where things actually break

### 5.1 The fragile-state vector

Food import dependence × FX weakness × political instability × hard currency reserves. The worst-positioned countries for a 2026-27 food price shock:

- **Egypt**: largest wheat importer globally; currency in chronic crisis; subsidized bread program is a regime-stability cornerstone.
- **Pakistan**: ~30% of GDP from agriculture, but a wheat importer; FX reserves perennially stressed.
- **Bangladesh**: heavy rice and edible-oil importer; large textile sector exposed to consumer compression.
- **Sub-Saharan Africa (Sahel + Horn of Africa)**: structurally food-insecure; already at famine conditions in Sudan, parts of Somalia and DRC.
- **Indonesia, Philippines, Vietnam**: rice/palm oil importers/exporters in complex balances.
- **Yemen, Lebanon, Syria**: chronic.

These aren't trade ideas directly (no liquid markets), but they shape the *political* response that determines whether the US/EU/China activate emergency food aid, export bans, or strategic reserves.

### 5.2 Export ban dynamics

The single biggest "tail" amplifier in any food crisis is export bans. The 2007-08 and 2010-11 spikes were *caused* more by export bans than by the underlying shortfalls:

- **India**: ban rice exports (did this in 2022); ban wheat exports (did this in 2022)
- **Indonesia**: ban palm oil exports (did this in 2022 — caused immediate global crisis, reversed in weeks)
- **Russia**: ban wheat exports (did this in 2010)
- **Vietnam**: ban rice exports (did this in 2008)

If you see an Indonesia palm oil export ban headline in 2026-27, palm oil moves 30-50% in a week. This is the kind of tail that's hard to hedge directly (no liquid US palm oil options) but bleeds into adjacent markets — soybean oil, rapeseed oil — that *are* tradable.

### 5.3 Strategic stockpiles and the China factor

China holds an estimated **50% of global grain reserves** (wheat, corn, rice combined). This is poorly documented, deliberately opaque, and structurally important:

- If China releases reserves, it could mute a global price spike for 6-12 months.
- If China *adds* to reserves on weather concerns (it did this in 2020-21, contributing to the run-up), it amplifies the spike.

This is a non-trivial reason that ag commodity prices don't always move the way fundamentals suggest. The Chinese state is the dominant marginal buyer in soybeans (60% of global trade), and significant in corn, wheat, and pork.

### 5.4 Indian monsoon and the demand-side feedback

India's domestic food situation is the world's swing factor on rice (60% of global rice exports until the 2022 ban, now ~40%). A weak monsoon in 2026 → Indian rice export ban → 5-10% of global rice supply removed overnight. Rice is the most concentrated and most ban-prone of the major grains.

The Indian Meteorological Department's monsoon forecast (published April for the June-September monsoon) is the single highest-information event in the global food calendar. **If May-June 2026 monsoon forecasts trend below 90% of long-term mean, that is the trigger.**

## 6. The Ackman framework — applied properly

### 6.1 What Ackman actually did (read the trade, not the narrative)

Both Ackman trades share a precise structure:

1. **Identify a state change** that the world is treating as continuous. (COVID = systemic risk priced as a flu; inflation = transitory priced as zero.)
2. **Find the cheapest instrument that pays asymmetrically on that state change**. Not the obvious one — the *cheap* one.
   - COVID: not equity puts (already bid up). CDS on IG/HY indices (default insurance) at near-record-tight spreads.
   - Inflation: not commodity longs. Interest rate options (caps/swaptions) on the 2-year, struck OTM, at near-zero implied vol.
3. **Size by premium, not by notional.** ~$27M of premium for both trades. <0.1% of fund AUM.
4. **Liquidate into something beaten-down once the catalyst hits.** Take the convex payoff, reinvest in stocks down 30%.
5. **Don't average down. Don't roll. Don't extend the trade. The asymmetry is the alpha.**

### 6.2 What the equivalent looks like for an El Niño thesis

The textbook Ackman move is **not** to buy DBA Jan 2027 calls (as analyzed in [dba-el-nino.md](dba-el-nino.md)). DBA is broad, mostly weighted to grains, and didn't pop materially in 2015-16 or 2023-24. It's the "buy S&P puts" version of the trade — not cheap enough and not convex enough.

The Ackman-style instrument set:

#### Tier 1: Concentrated tropical soft commodity calls

- **Cocoa futures (CC)** — already moved in 2024, but a fresh El Niño + low West African inventories could drive another leg. ETFs: NIB (discontinued), CHOC (London-listed). Best access: cocoa futures or options directly via futures broker.
- **Coffee (Robusta KC/RC)** — Vietnamese drought sensitivity is high.
- **Sugar (SB / #11)** — Brazilian center-south + Indian monsoon co-determine.
- **Palm oil** — hard to access from US accounts. CPO futures in Bursa Malaysia. Proxies: soybean oil (BO) trades inversely correlated to palm in panic regimes.

For each: **out-of-money calls 12-18 months out**, struck 30-50% above spot, at IVs of 25-35%. Sizes 0.5-1% of portfolio each. Premium-defined risk.

#### Tier 2: Fertilizer / ag-input equity convexity

- **Mosaic (MOS)** — phosphate + potash. Trades on commodity prices; high beta to fertilizer cycles.
- **Nutrien (NTR)** — potash + nitrogen + retail; the diversified play.
- **CF Industries (CF)** — pure-play nitrogen/urea. Most direct Hormuz beneficiary.
- **OCI Global** — pure-play nitrogen, more international.
- **Corteva (CTVA)** — seeds + crop protection. Indirect, but operating leverage to farm income.

Trade: **OTM calls Jan-2027 or Jan-2028**, struck 20-30% above spot, at 6-12 month tenor for cheaper theta. Or stock + tight stop if you want spot exposure. CF in particular has been a 2× / 0.4× stock multiple times in past cycles.

#### Tier 3: The "interest-rate option" equivalents — short food-margin compression

- **Long-dated puts on food retailers / restaurants**: KR, WMT, COST, MCD, SBUX, CMG, YUM. Margins compress when input costs rise faster than they can pass through.
- **Specifically**: **Hershey (HSY)** if cocoa pops again. Mondelez (MDLZ). Restaurant Brands (QSR). These are direct, single-commodity exposures.

Buy the SAFE single-stock puts at 6-12 month tenor, struck 10-15% OTM. These are the "interest rate caps" of the food complex — convex, defined-risk, and currently cheap because the market thinks inflation is over.

#### Tier 4: EM / fragile state hedges (advanced)

- Short EGX (Egypt), short PKR (Pakistan currency NDF), short Sri Lankan or Pakistani sovereign credit — only via macro/EM platforms. These are 100-bagger payoffs in a true food-crisis tail but require institutional access.

#### Tier 5: The "beaten-down equity" reinvestment list (post-payoff)

If/when the convex book pays — say it 5x's into late 2027 — what do you redeploy into?

- **Ag equipment** (Deere DE, AGCO, CNH Industrial) — operating leverage to farm income. These tend to lag commodity peaks by 12-18 months. **The right post-El-Niño long.**
- **Seeds / crop protection** (Corteva CTVA, FMC).
- **Domestic / non-disrupted refiners and food processors** (ADM, BG, Tyson, Pilgrim's Pride).
- **Water rights / farmland** (LAND, FPI) — long-cycle, not great public vehicles.
- **Selectively beaten-down developed-market equities** if there's a broader food-inflation-driven equity correction (similar to Ackman's S&P buyback in Mar 2020).

### 6.3 Sizing — Ackman discipline

The whole point of the Ackman framework is that the premium is small enough to be totally fine losing 100% of, and the payoff is large enough that even a 30% probability of success has positive EV.

If your portfolio is $X and you want to follow this framework:

- **Total convex book**: 1-3% of $X across all tiers. So on $100k, $1-3k. On $1M, $10-30k.
- **Concentration**: 0.3-1% per single position. So 3-8 positions, no more.
- **Tenor**: Mix of 6-month and 18-month. Don't get caught in a single expiry window.
- **Re-up rule**: If the thesis bleeds through Q3 2026 without triggering, you re-evaluate — don't reflexively roll.

This is genuinely Ackman-shape: small premium, defined risk, convex payoff, mechanical sizing.

## 7. The two-sided risk: what kills this trade

### 7.1 The neutral-to-weak ENSO outcome

If NOAA's 2-in-3 odds resolve into the 1-in-3 alternative — neutral or weak El Niño — then:
- Cocoa supply recovers as West African production normalizes.
- Coffee retraces from 2024 highs.
- Sugar fundamentals dominate (Brazilian ethanol vs. sugar mix).
- Palm oil flat.
- Fertilizer crunch becomes the only story; corn might move on supply but moderately.

In this world, the convex book bleeds out over 6-12 months and you take the premium as a tuition payment.

### 7.2 The "ban averted, China sells reserves" outcome

If China does what it did in 2008 (released grain reserves to mute the price spike) or the US/Russia/EU coordinate emergency wheat releases, you can get a 30% deficit in production absorbed by 5% draws on reserves. Prices stay flat. The convex book bleeds out.

### 7.3 The shipping/insurance disaster outcome

If the Strait of Hormuz actually closes (not just disrupted — physically closed), the trade *over*-pays initially, then the world economy seizes up, deflation hits, and food demand collapses faster than supply. This is the 2008 dynamic — commodities spiked then crashed. The trade needs to be unwound at the spike, not held into the crash.

### 7.4 The "macro de-risking" outcome

If S&P -25%+ happens for unrelated reasons (recession, AI bubble pop, geopolitics) before the El Niño thesis triggers, you lose the implicit "have capital to redeploy" optionality. The Ackman 2020 trade worked because he had dry powder to buy the bottom. If your portfolio is already drawn down, the post-payoff redeployment doesn't have the same firepower.
